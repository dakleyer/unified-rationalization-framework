# Unified Rationalization Framework — G0.11

## 1. Purpose

Unified rationalization is a way to reach one coherent, accountable decision about a real capability when several professions, systems, organisations, or jurisdictions are involved. It does not require one universal process, vendor, database, or compulsory sequence.

The capability under review may depend on people, craft, local knowledge, processes, information, applications, infrastructure, suppliers, contracts, and legal responsibilities together. A system that appears redundant in a technology inventory may protect a distinctive skill or essential service. Conversely, an apparently innovative AI initiative may reproduce an existing capability at higher lifecycle cost.

The framework therefore begins with the outcome and the whole situation, not with a preset elimination target or a presumed technology purchase.

## 2. Entry points

The same decision can begin in three different ways:

| Entry mode | Typical trigger | First decision question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom-up or opportunity-led | Hidden expertise, local practice, redundant services, or unused capability found in the ecosystem | What can be protected, improved, reused, or scaled before sourcing something new? |
| Demand-based | A new product, market, compliance requirement, public service, or delivery backlog | Can an existing capability satisfy the demand in another site or organisation, with appropriate adaptation? |
| Top-down | Strategy, portfolio investment, governance, merger, expansion, or transformation | Which capability choices deliver the target outcomes with acceptable dependencies, rights, and responsibilities? |

The framework connects these entry points so their consequences can be compared without centralising every decision.

## 3. The object of review

The object is the capacity to achieve a meaningful outcome. The review uses seven connected views only to the degree relevant to the decision:

| View | What may need to be seen |
| --- | --- |
| Value and demand | Customers or beneficiaries, products, required service levels, economic and public value, and the value that could be lost or extended |
| People and knowledge | Skills, tacit routines, craft, team capacity, handovers, working relationships, human effects, and local distinctive value |
| Process and service | End-to-end work, exceptions, variations, inputs, outputs, operating responsibilities, and service continuity |
| Information and applications | Shared meanings, data objects, records, provenance, rights, applications, ownership, interfaces, APIs, protocols, and integration cost |
| Technology and resilience | Platforms, infrastructure, vendors, security, operating cost, reliability, rollback, and continuity constraints |
| Authority and obligations | Decision rights, affected people, contracts, privacy, labour, accessibility, safety, sector rules, and jurisdiction |
| Origin and recipient | The organisation or community where value exists, the proposed receiving organisation, transfer conditions, and the recipient's capacity to sustain it |

These views are not seven separate compliance exercises. They give the people involved a shared description of the same choice.

## 4. Decision range

Rationalization is not synonymous with cost cutting or application removal. The result may be to:

- preserve a valuable capability;
- improve it in place;
- reuse or replicate it;
- scale or federate it across organisations;
- integrate it with other services or systems;
- consolidate genuine duplication;
- introduce AI for a defined need;
- change or retire what no longer serves its purpose; or
- defer with a stated reason, owner, evidence gap, and review date.

The options must remain visible together. A decision to preserve or scale what already works is as legitimate as a decision to replace it.

## 5. Five-question review

### Q1. Why act?

State the problem or opportunity, who needs the outcome, what baseline exists, and how success would be recognised. An AI product is a possible answer, never the premise.

### Q2. What already exists?

Look for working capability, local practice, data, infrastructure, portfolio records, skills, supplier commitments, and related decisions. Reuse existing evidence rather than repeating discovery.

### Q3. What options are credible?

Compare the complete decision range. Include non-AI alternatives, the option not to change, and the possibility of adapting a capability from another origin.

### Q4. What remains unclear or unsafe?

Resolve only gaps that materially affect the choice. Route specialised questions to competent legal, safety, privacy, cybersecurity, labour, accessibility, financial, architectural, or sector-specific processes. The framework records the handoff; it does not claim to replace those regimes.

### Q5. Who decides and sustains the result?

Identify legitimate decision owners, beneficiaries, implementation responsibilities, dependencies, authorised boundaries, oversight, escalation conditions, and a review point.

## 6. Proportionality

A small and reversible decision should not require the machinery of a major restructuring. A high-impact or cross-jurisdictional change must still meet the full obligations that apply to it.

Proportionate means gathering enough evidence for a defensible choice while avoiding duplicate inventories, committees, and approval layers. It never means weakening privacy, labour rights, accessibility, safety, cybersecurity, public accountability, or sector law.

## 7. Board and executive tests

The five questions structure the inquiry. The six tests introduced through Andrea Slingsby's independent contribution state what a board or executive committee should see before approving capital and while tracking sustained value:

1. **Problem:** What business or public outcome is being pursued, against which baseline and measures?
2. **Alternatives:** Were preservation, improvement, reuse, scaling, consolidation, replacement, non-AI options, and justified deferral compared?
3. **Value:** Where will end-to-end benefits arise after full lifecycle cost, dependencies, bottlenecks, and impacts on customers, employees, and other functions?
4. **Execution:** Is the organisation ready in leadership, workforce, data, integration, supplier arrangements, and operating capacity?
5. **Accountability:** Who owns the outcome, which decisions are authorised, where must humans intervene, and when must the matter escalate?
6. **Continuation:** Which milestones, assurance requirements, and stopping conditions govern further investment, correction, or withdrawal?

These tests belong in existing board papers, investment decisions, and risk reporting. They should not become a parallel approval bureaucracy.

## 8. Lifecycle use

The framework is most valuable before procurement, model selection, or deployment, but the decision remains live:

1. **Explore and frame:** establish the real outcome, capability, affected people, and decision authority.
2. **Compare and decide:** reuse evidence, examine alternatives, address material gaps, and record the accountable disposition.
3. **Design and acquire:** pass the selected option and its context into the appropriate architecture, procurement, risk, legal, and technical processes.
4. **Implement and operate:** maintain ownership, authorised scope, significant exceptions, dependencies, and benefits accountability.
5. **Review and adapt:** continue, improve, scale, change, stop, or retire according to the evidence and review conditions.

## 9. Minimum decision record

A proportionate record should identify:

- the outcome and baseline;
- the capability and material context;
- the alternatives considered and reasons for the chosen disposition;
- evidence reused and important unknowns;
- applicable specialist or statutory handoffs;
- people and organisations affected;
- origin, recipient, and transfer conditions where relevant;
- decision owner, implementation owner, and authorised boundaries;
- lifecycle costs, dependencies, benefits, and risks that materially affect the decision; and
- review date, milestones, escalation, correction, and stopping conditions.

## 10. Relationship with existing methods

The framework is an integrating perspective. It can work with application-portfolio management, enterprise architecture, capability management, process improvement, investment governance, COBIT, TOGAF and ArchiMate, ISO enterprise-modelling and governance guidance, NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and 42005, the OECD AI Principles, and applicable law.

Its specific role is upstream and cross-disciplinary: identify what the decision actually concerns, bring together only the existing methods and evidence it requires, keep the full range of choices open, and hand selected options into the regimes that properly govern them.

## 11. Evaluation agenda

G0.11 is suitable for structured field evaluation, not a claim of completed validation. Comparative work should examine whether the method:

- improves the quality and traceability of capability decisions;
- exposes material dependencies, rights, and risks earlier;
- reduces duplicate discovery and approval work;
- protects valuable knowledge and services that narrow inventories overlook;
- improves capital allocation and sustained benefits realisation;
- helps organisations distinguish a justified AI use from a better non-AI alternative; and
- transfers useful capability across organisations without erasing local context or responsibility.

Evaluation should include counterexamples, failed transfers, sector-specific constraints, and cases where an existing method already provides a sufficient answer.
